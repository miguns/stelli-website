Early Spaying and Neutering of Kittens

Is it safe, can it affect growth, and why is it done at such a young age in the first place?

Early spaying and neutering of kittens is a topic that still raises plenty of questions. Is it safe? Could it affect growth? Why is it even done at such a young age?

The point is certainly not to fix every kitten as early as possible. For kittens intended for breeding, the situation is naturally different. But for kittens going to their new homes as pets, early spay/neuter can be a sensible way to prevent unwanted litters and some of the problems that come with sexual maturity.

What is early spay/neuter?

Early, or paediatric, spay/neuter means fixing a cat before it reaches sexual maturity. In practice, that means the procedure is done on a noticeably younger animal than used to be common.

The main reason is that cats can become sexually active quite early. Current recommendations from the American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) support neutering cats not intended for breeding by 5 months of age. At the same time, they stress that the exact timing should always be assessed by a veterinarian based on the individual kitten's health and condition.

So early spay/neuter doesn't mean "fix it as early as possible no matter what." What matters is that the kitten is healthy, thriving, and that the procedure is carried out by a vet experienced with young patients.

Why is it done in such young cats?

The most common reason is preventing unwanted litters.

With a kitten going to a pet home, we as breeders can be sure it won't later become a parent to an unplanned litter. At the same time, the new family doesn't have to figure out exactly when the first heat will happen, when to schedule the procedure, or how to prevent an unwanted mating.

Neutering isn't only about reproduction, though.

Unneutered cats also carry health and behavioural risks linked to sex hormones. In females, one of the most serious issues is pyometra, a bacterial infection of the uterus that can be life-threatening and requires urgent treatment. Neutering prevents this condition.

In unneutered males, spraying, roaming and conflicts with other males tend to be more common. Fights can lead to bites, abscesses and other infections. For males with outdoor access, the risk of injury and certain infectious diseases increases as well.

Of course, this doesn't mean every unneutered male will spray or fight, or that every unneutered female will develop pyometra. These are known risks tied to sexual maturity and lifestyle.

Is early spay/neuter safe?

A young age alone doesn't make the procedure unsuitable for a kitten.

As with any surgery, though, it's important that the animal is healthy and in good condition beforehand. A vet should assess the kitten's overall health, size and condition in advance and decide whether the procedure is appropriate at that particular time.

The veterinary practice's experience with anaesthesia and surgery in young cats also plays a big role.

Early spay/neuter isn't a race to the lowest possible age. What matters more than the number itself is a healthy kitten, the right timing, and an experienced vet.

Does early spay/neuter affect a cat's growth and development?

This is one of the most common concerns.

Available studies in kittens don't show that early spay/neuter leads to smaller size or poorer physical development. In one controlled study, cats neutered at 7 weeks, at 7 months, and cats left intact were compared. No clinically significant differences were found between the cats neutered at 7 weeks and at 7 months across the physical or behavioural development parameters studied.

Neutering can affect the timing of growth plate closure, but that alone doesn't mean a cat will grow up smaller or develop worse.

And what about our own experience?

In our own cattery, we've actually had kittens that were neutered and went on to grow larger than their littermates who stayed intact and were kept for breeding. We've heard similar experiences from a number of other breeders too.

Of course, that doesn't mean neutering causes bigger size as a rule. A cat's size is shaped mainly by genetics, nutrition, health and overall development.

What our experience does show is that there's no basis for the fear that an early-neutered kitten has to stay small or won't develop properly.

How does it work with our kittens?

For us, the individual kitten's health always comes first.

Before the procedure, a vet examines the kitten. If it's healthy, thriving, and its overall condition is suitable for surgery, neutering can be carried out before it leaves for its new family.

The exact timing and suitability of the procedure is always assessed by the vet. We don't go by the age on the calendar alone, but above all by how the individual kitten is developing.

Our goal isn't to have a neutered kitten at any cost. Our goal is to hand over a healthy, well-socialised and happy kitten to its new family, with the procedure carried out safely and at the right time.

Early spay/neuter in cattery practice

Early spay/neuter isn't something that only applies to shelters or rescue organisations. You'll find it in catteries like ours too.

The reason is simple. The kitten leaves for its new family already without the risk of unwanted breeding, and the new owners don't have to deal with neutering later on.

In conclusion

Early spay/neuter doesn't have to be a cause for concern.

What matters most is that the kitten is healthy, thriving, and that the procedure is carried out by an experienced vet at the right time. The available data doesn't support the idea that early spay/neuter by itself stunts growth or causes poorer development.

For kittens not intended for breeding, early spay/neuter can also be a practical way to prevent unwanted litters and some of the problems that come with sexual maturity.

And as with everything concerning a cat's health, one important rule applies: every kitten is an individual, and the final word should belong to the vet who knows its health.
