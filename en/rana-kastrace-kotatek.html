Early Spaying and Neutering of Kittens

Is it safe, can it affect growth, and why is it done at such a young age in the first place?

Early spaying and neutering of kittens is a topic that still raises plenty of questions. Is it safe? Could it affect growth and development? And why is neutering sometimes done in such young cats in the first place?

Let's break it down a little.

What is early spay/neuter?

Early, or paediatric, spay/neuter means fixing a cat before it reaches sexual maturity.

The American Association of Feline Practitioners (AAFP) supports neutering cats not intended for breeding by 5 months of age. Part of the reason is that a cat can become reproductively active as early as 4-5 months, even without obvious outward signs of sexual maturity. AAFP also stresses assessing the individual animal's health and the vet's experience with surgery and anaesthesia in young patients.

What matters is that the kitten is healthy, thriving, and that the procedure is carried out appropriately.

Why is it done in such young cats?

The most common reason is preventing unwanted litters.

With a kitten going to a pet home, we as breeders can be sure there won't later be an unplanned mating. At the same time, the new family doesn't have to work out when the cat will reach sexual maturity, when to schedule neutering, or how to prevent an unwanted mating.

Neutering isn't only about reproduction, though.

Unneutered cats also carry health and behavioural issues linked to sex hormones. In females, one significant health problem is pyometra, an infection of the uterus. It's a serious condition that can lead to systemic infection and further complications, and it requires prompt veterinary care.

Removing the ovaries and uterus during neutering prevents pyometra, since it removes the reproductive organs in which the condition develops.

In unneutered males, sexual activity can be linked to behaviours such as spraying, roaming or conflicts with other males. In cats with outdoor access, these conflicts can also mean a higher risk of bites, abscesses and other injuries.

Of course, this doesn't mean every unneutered male will spray or fight. These are possible consequences of sexual maturity and a particular cat's lifestyle.

Is early spay/neuter safe?

The safety of early spay/neuter has been examined in a number of veterinary studies.

One of the larger prospective studies included 775 cats, divided by age at neutering into three groups: under 12 weeks, 12-23 weeks, and 24 weeks or older. The researchers tracked complications during anaesthesia, surgery and the first seven days afterwards.

The rate of serious complications didn't differ significantly between the age groups. Cats younger than 12 weeks showed no higher rate of complications than the older groups. The authors concluded that prepubertal neutering, with appropriate anaesthetic and surgical care, didn't increase short-term morbidity or mortality.

Another study directly compared neutering kittens at 8-12 weeks of age with neutering at 6-8 months. Both groups had very few surgical complications, and early spay/neuter wasn't associated with a higher rate of postoperative complications.

Of course, this doesn't mean neutering is risk-free. Every surgical procedure and every anaesthetic carries some risk. In young animals, particular attention also needs to go to things like body temperature, anaesthetic dosing and overall patient monitoring.

That's why the individual kitten's health and the veterinary practice's experience both matter.

Does early spay/neuter affect growth?

Concern about growth is one of the most common arguments against early spay/neuter.

Available studies, though, don't support the idea that prepubertal neutering stunts growth or leads to poorer physical development.

In one controlled study, cats were split into three groups: neutered at 7 weeks, neutered at 7 months, and a group left intact. Among other things, the researchers tracked body weight, growth plate closure and some behavioural characteristics.

No differences were found in the physical or behavioural development parameters studied between the cats neutered at 7 weeks and at 7 months. Among other findings, intact cats had earlier closure of some growth plates.

So neutering can affect the timing of growth plate closure. Their closure may happen later than in sexually intact cats. That alone, though, doesn't mean the cat will end up smaller or develop worse.

The research mainly points to a change in growth plate closure timing, not a halt to growth itself.

What do longer-term studies show?

Early spay/neuter hasn't only been studied in the short term.

A prospective study of 800 kittens compared kittens neutered at roughly 8-12 weeks with kittens whose neutering was delayed to 6-8 months. The study tracked health problems in both the short and longer term, and was designed specifically to test the concerns raised about prepubertal neutering.

Another long-term study followed 263 cats neutered at either an early or a more traditional age.

An even larger retrospective study included 1,660 cats and tracked their health and behaviour for up to 11 years after neutering. Cats neutered before 5.5 months showed no increased rate of death, relinquishment to a shelter, or serious health or behavioural problems compared with cats neutered later.

So the research behind early spay/neuter doesn't rest on a single old experiment. There are studies covering the safety of the procedure, growth, development and longer-term health outcomes.

Current recommendations from veterinary professional organisations support neutering cats not intended for breeding by 5 months of age. This is supported by bodies including the AAFP and AVMA, among others.

The AVMA also stresses that the exact timing should be assessed by a vet based on current scientific literature and the individual animal's health.

And what about a cat's size?

An adult cat's size isn't determined solely by whether or when it was neutered.

Genetics, breed, sex, nutrition, health and overall growth all play a significant role.

In our own cattery, for instance, we've had kittens that were neutered and went on to grow larger than their littermates who stayed intact and were kept for breeding. We've heard similar experiences from other breeders too.

Individual cases obviously can't be turned into a scientific conclusion, and we're certainly not claiming that neutering causes bigger size. What our experience does show is that there's no basis for the idea that an early-neutered kitten has to stay small.

What matters after neutering?

Neutering also changes a cat's energy needs.

The risk of being overweight increases in cats after neutering, so it's important to keep an eye on body condition, food intake and exercise.

A more recent long-term study followed more than 2,000 cats and compared their body weight and body condition depending on the age at neutering. Cats neutered at 4 months or earlier, at 5 months, and at 6 months showed no differences in body condition or weight that pointed to a significant disadvantage for any of these timings.

After neutering, it's therefore important to set up feeding appropriately and regularly check the cat's condition. According to this study, the age at neutering itself doesn't explain differences in body condition between cats neutered at 4, 5 or 6 months.

How does it work with our kittens?

For us, the individual kitten's health always comes first.

Before the procedure, a vet examines the kitten. If it's healthy, thriving, and its overall condition is suitable for surgery, neutering can be carried out before it leaves for its new family.

The exact timing and suitability of the procedure is always assessed by the vet. We don't go by the age on the calendar alone, but above all by the individual kitten's health and development.

Our goal isn't to have a neutered kitten at any cost. Our goal is to hand over a healthy, well-socialised and happy kitten to its new family, with the procedure carried out safely and at the right time.

Early spay/neuter in cattery practice

Early spay/neuter isn't a practice used only by shelters or rescue organisations. Some breeders use it too.

The reason is practical. The kitten leaves for its new family already unable to reproduce, and the new owner doesn't later have to deal with scheduling neutering or the risk of an unwanted mating.

At the same time, it clearly separates a kitten intended for breeding from one sold as a pet.

In conclusion

Early spay/neuter isn't a risk-free procedure, just as no surgery under general anaesthesia is risk-free. Available studies, though, don't support the idea that a young age by itself leads to stunted growth, poorer physical development, or a higher rate of serious complications.

In healthy kittens, early spay/neuter can be carried out safely with the right anaesthetic and surgical approach, and current recommendations from veterinary professional organisations support it for cats not intended for breeding.

For us, what matters most is why a kitten is being neutered, whether the procedure is appropriate for it at that particular time, and whether it's carried out by an experienced vet.

Every kitten is an individual, and the final decision on the suitability and timing of the procedure should always be made by a vet based on that particular animal's health.
